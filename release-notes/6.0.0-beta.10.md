# WiCAN PRO 6.0.0-beta.10

Supported device: [WiCAN PRO](https://www.crowdsupply.com/meatpi-electronics/wican-pro) only. Home Assistant: v6 requires the [meatpi-multi-device](https://github.com/meatpiHQ/ha-wican/tree/meatpi-multi-device) branch of the ha-wican integration.

## Before you update
<!-- release.py:layout -->
<!-- /release.py:layout -->
- A device that ran 6.0.0-beta.5 has its OBD chip on firmware V2.3.22. Open Quick Setup's vehicle step (or System > Maintenance) after the update and let it write V2.3.24: until then, an app that uses the automatic OBD protocol only connects after the device has been unplugged and plugged back in, and a restart of the device breaks it again.
- Running the Audi Q4 e-tron, Skoda Enyaq or Elroq, VW ID or Porsche Taycan profile on 6.0.0-beta.1 to beta.6: update now, or poll the standard PIDs only until you do. Those profiles made the OBD chip write its EEPROM on every poll cycle (fixed in beta.7), and on a chip written that often the reset WiCAN sent at every wake from sleep could destroy what the chip stores (issue #1023, the bug fix "The OBD chip is no longer reset while it starts up" below).
- Coming from a v6 alfa or beta: settings, profiles and files are kept; the standard PID rows are re-numbered at the first start. A custom PID you wrote on a v6 alfa or beta against a single-frame reply reads one byte too far: run Test a PID (B0 is the first byte after the CAN id) and move each B index up by one, or re-import the published profile.
- A beta.1 device that parked itself (LED breathing red) updates from safe mode: hold the button until the LED turns sky blue, then yellow, join the device's own access point (its name starts with `WiCAN_`, password `@meatpi#`), open `http://192.168.0.10` and upload the firmware there. Settings are kept.
- Coming from firmware 4.x: the stored files, logs and the saved configuration are erased by the new partition layout; download a backup first (System > Maintenance > Configuration backup). The device restarts once more on its first start.
- In case of a boot loop, perform a [factory reset](https://meatpihq.github.io/wican-fw/factory-reset/factory-reset).

## New since beta.9
- **The car's name at the top of every page.** The header shows the name of the car WiCAN is reading (the name given in Quick Setup) above the device id and the address, and the browser tab reads the name too, so several WiCANs in several tabs can be told apart. When WiCAN moves to another car it knows, the name follows. A click on the name renames the car: the new name is saved with Submit Changes and the device restarts, like a setting. A device with no car set up looks as before.
- **PIDs on MS CAN, single-wire CAN and CH CAN (WiCAN PRO).** A custom or vehicle-specific PID on the Automate page has a new field, Bus: the car's OBD protocol (as before, the default), MS CAN (pins 3 and 11, 125 kbit/s: the body bus of many Ford and Mazda cars), single-wire CAN (pin 1, 33.3 kbit/s: GM) or CH CAN (pins 12 and 13, 500 kbit/s). For a car whose bus runs at another speed there is a Bus speed next to it (500, 250, 125, 100, 95.2, 83.3, 50 or 33.3 kbit/s). WiCAN asks the PIDs of one bus together, one bus after the other, so a round changes bus once per bus and not once per PID, and the page shows how the round goes. The Test button asks on the PID's bus. Nothing is stored in the OBD chip for it: the chip is taken to the other bus in its working memory only and is back on the car's OBD protocol as soon as no PID of another bus is due, so an app or a scan that comes next finds it where it always was. A PID on another bus must not pick a protocol in its own init (no `ATSP`, `ATTP`, `STP`, `VTP`): the Bus field is the whole choice. If nobody answers on a bus at the chosen speed (the chip reports `CAN ERROR` three times in a row), its PIDs rest for 30 seconds before the next try, so a wrong speed does not keep disturbing a live bus; the page marks the resting bus, says what to check and counts down to the next try. While polling is paused by the battery voltage (a parked car), nothing is asked on these buses either, so their modules are not kept awake. 11-bit ids only for now.
- **Cars from before CAN: ISO 9141-2, KWP2000 and J1850 (WiCAN PRO).** Until this version WiCAN's own functions (Detect my vehicle, the Test button, the polling of the Automate page) worked on CAN cars only. A car that talks on the K-line (pin 7: ISO 9141-2 and ISO 14230-4 "KWP2000", most European and Asian cars from before about 2008) or on SAE J1850 (pin 2: older GM, Ford and Chrysler) could be read through an ELM327 app on WiCAN but not by WiCAN itself (issue #1045, a 2000 Toyota 4Runner and a 2005 Porsche Cayman S). Now it can. Detect my vehicle goes on to SAE J1850 and the K-line when no CAN protocol answers and finds the car, its VIN and its supported standard PIDs there. WiCAN also finds such a car by itself: plugged into one with the engine running, it looks on these protocols once nothing answers on CAN and nobody else is talking on the CAN pins, usually within half a minute of its start, and a car it has never seen is detected and stored right there. Under Automate, the OBD protocol setting of the standard PIDs offers all nine OBD-II protocols, so a car can also be set by hand. The standard PIDs and your own expressions read such a car's replies exactly as on CAN (`[B3:B4]/4` is the engine speed on every protocol), so the standard set and a PID copied from a CAN car need no change. An expression you wrote for such a car against the Test button of firmware 4.50, which counted the car's two header bytes as data (`[B5:B6]/4` for the engine speed, `B5-40` for the coolant), has to be changed to the CAN numbers (`[B3:B4]/4`, `B3-40`). A K-line car has to be woken up before it answers: the first request takes about three seconds (under one second on cars with the fast start), every request after it about 0.2 seconds. The car ends the connection by itself after five seconds without a request; with a slow PID table WiCAN sends one short request in between so that it does not have to wake the car again in every round, and the Test button waits for the wake-up instead of giving up. When the ignition was off and comes back on, polling picks up again by itself within a few seconds. The trouble code check (off until you switch it on) reads and clears the codes of such a car as on CAN: stored and pending codes, the lamp, the freeze frame, however many codes the car holds (these protocols send three codes per message, and all messages are read). If you choose one of the protocols 1 to 5 and later go back to an earlier firmware version, that version does not know the choice and puts the Automate settings back to their defaults; the PID tables are kept.

## Bug fixes since beta.9
- **Tailscale: a crash some tens of seconds after the network dropped.** When the Wi-Fi went away while WiCAN was reconnecting to a Tailscale relay, WiCAN shut its Tailscale client down, waited 30 seconds for the client's tasks to finish and then freed the client's memory, even when one task was still busy with a connection attempt (a single attempt into a network that just went away can take a minute). That task went on running on memory that belonged to something else by then, and the device crashed a little later, usually with a memory fault in the network code. WiCAN now keeps the client until every one of its tasks has finished, however long that takes, and only then starts the next one; a relay connection is no longer retried once the shutdown has begun. Until the old client is gone the VPN reads "waiting" instead of "connected". No setting and no memory size is changed.
- **Tailscale: memory lost every time the VPN went down and came back.** Each time WiCAN's Tailscale client was stopped and started again (a Wi-Fi drop is enough), about 17 KB of memory stayed behind. When the client was restarted while its relay connection was still up (for instance after the control server had been unreachable for 30 seconds), the old relay connections were not closed either and kept their memory and their network connections (on the bench about 280 KB over four restarts). None of it came back before the next reboot, so a WiCAN that stays awake for weeks on a flaky network could run out. Both are released now: the free memory is the same after four restarts in a row. An unreachable Tailscale control server is also no longer counted as an error in the device's log: it is retried, as before.
- **Tailscale is back within seconds of a network drop.** When the Wi-Fi went away while WiCAN was in the middle of a connection attempt to a Tailscale relay or to the control server, that attempt could not be given up: it ran for up to a minute, the Tailscale client could not be shut down before it ended, and the VPN came back about 70 seconds after the Wi-Fi did (on the bench in six of nine drops). A connection attempt is now given up after 10 seconds, and at once when the client is being shut down: the VPN is back about 15 seconds after the Wi-Fi.
- **J2534 battery voltage is measured, not assumed.** A PassThru tool that asks for the battery voltage (`READ_VBATT`) got an answer without a value from WiCAN, and 12.0 V from the Windows driver, which never asked. WiCAN now answers its calibrated battery reading in millivolts, for an open device, without a vehicle-bus channel; when it has no reading it says so instead of answering without a value. A Windows tool sees the measured value once the PassThru driver is updated too: driver 1.6 still answers 12.0 V by itself. The updated driver is not in the installer yet.
- **The OBD chip is no longer reset while it starts up (issue #1023: OBD chips that stopped answering for good).** This is the cause of the dead OBD chips reported since 6.0.0-beta.4. The chip tidies its own stored settings while it starts up, after it has stored a protocol a few hundred times, and a reset in that moment destroys what it stores: the chip loses its identity and its sleep setting, or it never answers again. Every time WiCAN woke from sleep it reset the chip 300 ms after waking it, which is exactly when the chip starts that work. Betas 4 to 6 also made the chip store a protocol at every switch of a vehicle profile that polls on two protocols (the VW group electric cars: ID.3, ID.4, Q4 e-tron, Enyaq, Elroq, Born, Taycan), so on those cars the chip had that work to do after every few minutes of driving. Firmware 4.x did neither. WiCAN now waits until the chip signals that it has started and is ready, and only then talks to it; it never sends a reset while the chip is starting. It also no longer uses the chip's reset line on a chip that answers: wherever it used to (after a restart whose first command got no clear answer, when it put the chip back to sleep, after repairing the chip's sleep setting, at the end of a chip firmware update) it now resets the chip with a command, which the chip carries out by itself at a safe moment. The reset line is kept for one case only, a chip that does not respond at all. A wake from sleep is also about a second faster. If you run beta.4, beta.5 or beta.6 with one of those profiles, update now. A chip that already answers nothing cannot be brought back by a firmware update.
- **OBD chip trouble is now kept as a fault code.** WiCAN has a list of fault codes that survives restarts and power cycles until it is cleared (System Monitor in the web UI, with a red marker in the page header while any is recorded; also `GET /api/faults` and `faults` on the console). On System Monitor a small info button beside a code now says in plain words what the code means and what to do about it. Until now nothing about the OBD chip ever reached it: a chip that woke up while WiCAN was asleep, or a chip that did not answer at all, left a few log lines that were gone after the next restart, and every wake from sleep is a restart. Four codes are new. `obd_chip_woke`: the chip was asleep and woke up again while WiCAN slept (WiCAN puts it back, as before). `obd_chip_no_sleep`: the chip could not be put to sleep at all. `obd_chip_silent`: the chip did not answer at start-up and WiCAN had to fall back on its reset line; the code says whether the chip came back. `obd_chip_damaged`: the chip works but has lost what it stores about itself. A healthy chip produces none of them. If you report a problem with the OBD chip, the fault list is the first thing worth sending.
- **A fault code that keeps coming back no longer writes the flash for good.** WiCAN stored a fault code's count once in every start-up in which the fault occurred, so a fault that returned at every start-up cost a small write of the flash each time, for as long as the device lived. The count now stops at 10 (System Monitor shows `10+`), and the writing stops with it. The code stays in the list until you clear it, as before.
- **A damaged OBD chip is no longer rewritten over and over.** A chip that was hit but still works answers the firmware's version question with an error. WiCAN took that for a chip to repair, rewrote the chip's firmware, started the chip again, asked again, and so on for as long as it was awake. The rewrite repairs nothing. Such a chip is now left alone and used as it is, its sleep setting is put back so that it sleeps with WiCAN again, and System > Maintenance and `GET /api/obd_chip` (`fw.damaged`) say that its stored identity is damaged.
- **A protocol preset in a PID's init no longer wears the OBD chip's memory.** The chip writes a protocol preset (`STP 53`, `VTP 2C3`) and a transceiver link word (`ATLNK..`) into its own memory every time it receives one, whatever was sent before it. A PID's init is sent again every time the poller comes round to that PID, so a preset there, until this version the only way to another CAN bus, was a write of the chip's memory at every poll round. WiCAN no longer sends these words from its own tables. The Automate page refuses them in a PID that is switched on and in the Test button, and says what to use instead: the PID's Bus field. A table saved with an earlier version is kept: a PID with such a word is switched off once, and its row says why and what to do. In one of the init fields above a table (standard, custom or vehicle) the word is kept as it was typed, but it is not sent, and the PIDs of that table on the car's OBD protocol are not asked while it is there: the header that follows such a word was meant for another bus and does not belong on the car's own. The field, the table under it and the dashboard say so. Take the word out of the field and choose the Bus of the PIDs that are on another bus; a PID whose Bus is chosen is asked right away. Nothing in the saved settings is changed by the update, so going back to an earlier version finds them as they were. `STPBR` and the other words that only set a bit rate are not presets and work as before. Apps on the ELM327 port are not affected.
- **A protocol switch is sent to the OBD chip only after the chip has confirmed that its memory is off.** Since beta.7 the firmware sends `ATM0` ahead of every protocol switch it makes itself (the poller, the scans, the UDS tool, J2534, the CAN self-test), so that the chip does not write the switch into its own non-volatile protocol store. The switch then went out whatever the chip had answered. When the chip did not take that `ATM0` (seen on the bench: two characters of an app's unfinished line still in the chip's input, which made the chip answer `?`), the switch was sent with the chip's memory in an unknown state, and with the memory on, as it is after any reset of the chip, the store was written. Now the switch is not sent unless the chip answered `OK`, and the requests that would have followed it wait until a switch has gone through: they would otherwise go out on the protocol, and at the bit rate, of whatever used the chip before. Polling picks up by itself once the chip answers. Test-a-PID says which of the two happened, and `GET /api/obd_chip` counts both (`eeprom_guard.memory_refused`, `requests_held`). Apps on the ELM327 port are not affected: their protection is the `ATM0` the firmware puts behind their reset, as before.
- **The OBD chip's stored protocol is written once per car at most, and not at all when the chip already holds it.** After a vehicle detection the firmware saves the car's protocol in the chip (a real `ATSP`, the one deliberate write of the chip's own memory). Two cases wrote more than needed. A new car on a protocol the chip already held got a write that changed nothing, for example every detection on a device whose vehicle list had been cleared. And when the chip's answer to the save was lost, the firmware did not know whether the write had happened and sent it again at the next detection, in the same boot and in every later one. The firmware now reads the chip's stored protocol at every start (one read, no write), sends the save only when the chip holds something else, and counts the one save of a boot when it goes out rather than when it is answered. After a lost answer the next start's read settles it: if the chip took the write nothing more is sent. `GET /api/obd_chip` shows the stored protocol and counts the saves that were not needed (`eeprom_guard.stored_protocol`, `protocol_kept`).
- **A vehicle profile's protocol is never written into the OBD chip.** The protocol the firmware saves in the chip is the car's own: the one a standard OBD-II request answered on. A car that answers no standard request is confirmed in Quick Setup by the requests of its vehicle profile, and the protocol named there is the first `ATSP` of the profile, which need not be the one the answering request used. That protocol was saved in the chip. On the bench a profile with `ATSP7` and `ATSP6` rows, as the Audi / VW MEB profile has, was confirmed by an `ATSP6` request and `ATSP7` was written. Such a car now leaves the chip's stored protocol as it is. Nothing changes for polling: every row of a profile sets its own protocol each time, in the chip's working memory only, and rows on `ATSP6` and rows on `ATSP7` work side by side whichever protocol the chip holds.
- **"Detect my vehicle" works while the rows of a vehicle profile are being polled.** Most vehicle profiles tell the OBD chip how to answer a long reply of their own ECU (`ATFCSH`, `ATFCSD`, `ATFCSM1`). That setting stayed in effect when the firmware asked its own questions: the car's VIN came back cut off after its first part. When only profile rows were polled (no standard PID ticked), the detection then failed with "no ECU response (ignition on?)". The check at every start that tells which car WiCAN is plugged into could miss the VIN the same way, and then went by the car's set of control units alone, which two cars of the same model share. The firmware now puts those settings back before each of its own requests and the profile's rows set them again for theirs.
- **WiCAN recognises the car by itself when the rows of a vehicle profile are all it can poll.** At every start WiCAN asks the car who it is (its VIN, its control units) once a poll was answered. On a device with no car set up, and on one whose car was silent when the device started, it asked on the protocol the OBD chip was on at that moment. When the poll that had just answered was a profile row with its own `ATSP7`, that was the row's protocol and not the car's: the question went out where the car does not answer standard requests, three times, and WiCAN gave up until the next start. The car was then not stored or recognised by itself (no name in the header, no standard PID list, a swapped car not noticed) and "Detect my vehicle" had to be pressed by hand. A profile with rows on both protocols, as the Audi / VW MEB profile has, got through only by chance. WiCAN now looks for the car with standard requests, on the row's protocol first and then on the others of the bus, and asks where one answers.
- **Quick Setup confirms a car by its vehicle profile when the profile's ECU answers on a 29-bit id.** A car that answers no standard request is confirmed in Quick Setup by the first requests of the profile chosen there. Replies from an ECU with a 29-bit id that is not one of the OBD standard's (the battery ECU of the VW group's MEB cars, and the profiles for Volvo, Geely, Zeekr, the Porsche Taycan, the GMC Sierra EV and the Honda Prologue) were not read as answers, so such a car was confirmed only by a later request of the profile on an 11-bit id, or the detection ended with "no ECU response (ignition on?)". Polling these profiles was not affected. With it: the three requests Quick Setup sends are now the profile's first three manufacturer requests. Standard PIDs listed in a profile are passed over there, because the profile's requests are only used after the standard requests stayed silent. The text of the step says what the detection does.
- **A changed PID table sends its own setup commands at once.** After the PID table was replaced while it was being polled (Save on the Automate page, a profile loaded), the row that landed on the position polled last was sent without its own init, on whatever the previous row had set. A table of several rows corrected itself at the next row. A table of a single row never did: its request kept going out with the previous row's settings (protocol, header, receive filter) and its value stayed empty until the next restart or scan.
- **Quick Setup never sends a vehicle profile's requests at a bit rate the vehicle bus does not run.** "Detect my vehicle" with a profile chosen falls back to the profile's own requests when no standard request is answered. Those went out on the profile's protocol whatever the bus was running: a car profile (500 kbit/s) chosen on a vehicle with a 250 kbit/s network that answers no OBD request, a J1939 truck for example, put requests at the wrong bit rate on that network. On the bench this destroyed the traffic for a fifth of a second (865 error frames) and drove another node into bus-off. Polled rows, test-a-PID and the trouble code scan ask the bus first; this path did not. It asks now: the profile's requests are not sent, and the step says why ("the profile sets protocol 6 (500 kbit/s) and the vehicle bus runs at 250 kbit/s: not sent") in place of "no ECU response (ignition on?)".
- **The OBD chip lets go of the vehicle bus when WiCAN restarts.** Since beta.7 a restart of WiCAN that did not cut its power (a settings change, a firmware update) left the OBD chip connected to the vehicle bus at the bit rate of its last session, although the firmware treated the chip as freshly reset and silent. In the same car this did no harm. On a network running at another bit rate (a device that stays powered while it is moved, a test bench that changes its bus) the chip corrupted every frame on that network: on the bench about 500 error frames a second and the other nodes driven to bus-off, while WiCAN's own bus guard had stopped all polling and not one byte had been sent to the chip. The start-up now closes the chip's protocol right after resetting it. It costs about 50 ms at every start.
- **A long answer of the OBD chip arrives complete.** The chip talks to WiCAN's processor at 2,000,000 baud. An answer of more than about 120 characters in one piece (the chip's parameter summary `ATPPS`, its sleep configuration `STSLCS`, its protocol tables: what a terminal app or a support report asks for) could arrive with a piece missing and a few wrong characters in its place, while the log read `rx overflow (fifo) - input flushed`. On the bench that was one such answer in 20 on an idle device and one in four while the web interface was in use. Replies to OBD and UDS requests were not affected: a short reply fits the processor's receive buffer whole, and a long one is printed frame by frame. Two things were against a long answer: the firmware started emptying that buffer only when it was nearly full, with 40 microseconds to spare, and the interrupt that does it waited behind the WiFi interrupts on the same processor core. The firmware now starts at a quarter of that fill, which leaves it 480 microseconds, and the interrupt runs on the other core. A long answer reaches an app in the same pieces as before. `GET /api/obd_chip` reports how full the buffer got since the start and how often it was emptied late (`uart.rx_fifo_peak`, `uart.rx_late` of `uart.rx_handovers`).
- **The start-up check of the OBD chip's firmware runs before the first poll.** At every start WiCAN asks the OBD chip for its firmware version and updates a chip that is too old. With polling enabled the poller could get to the chip first: the check then ran when the poller first paused, 2.5 to 3.5 seconds later on the bench, and it gives up for that start after 5 seconds without a line in the log, so on a car that keeps the chip busy longer at first an old chip's update would have waited for a later start. The check now has the chip before anything else may use it, and the first poll starts about a tenth of a second later. A check that still does not get the chip says so in the log.
- **A vehicle on a 250 kbit/s CAN bus is found with WiCAN's own CAN controller switched on.** WiCAN PRO has a CAN controller of its own beside the OBD chip (CAN settings, off by default). Switched on, it acknowledged the OBD chip's requests, and WiCAN took those for the vehicle's traffic: on a bus where nothing else was talking yet it decided that the vehicle bus ran at 500 kbit/s and stopped trying 250 kbit/s until its next restart. A vehicle on 250 kbit/s that was still asleep when WiCAN started, or one that says nothing until it is asked, was then never found, and the Test button on such a vehicle could stop with "the vehicle bus carries traffic at a bitrate that could not be read" after a few requests. WiCAN now tells its own requests from the vehicle's traffic: before the OBD chip may send, WiCAN listens to the CAN pins themselves while the chip is quiet, and only what is there then counts as the vehicle's. Still so: a CAN controller fixed at a bit rate other than the vehicle's cannot read that vehicle's bus, and when the bus carries traffic of its own WiCAN leaves it alone (nothing is sent). Where Automate shows why it is paused, it now names the controller's bit rate and the setting to change ("set the CAN bit rate to Automatic"); before, it said that the bus ran at neither 250 nor 500 kbit/s, which was not true of a 250 kbit/s bus. A WiCAN with the CAN controller off was not affected.
- **J2534 over J1850 VPW reaches the car.** A PassThru application that opened a J1850 VPW channel got its channel and had its messages accepted, but never a reply: the channel's set-up put the OBD chip back on the car's stored protocol right after choosing VPW, so the requests went out on CAN, and a set of receive filters behind it let no VPW reply through on this chip. Found with the first VPW node on the bench; both are corrected, and a functional request such as `68 6A F1 01 00` is answered `48 6B 10 41 00 ..` through the application's own PASS filter. Physical addressing was not tested.

## Bug fixes since beta.8
- **Tailscale: a push to another tailnet device's own address works without that device contacting WiCAN first, and comes back after an interruption.** The Home Assistant webhook, an HTTP destination or MQTT addressed to a tailnet device by its own Tailscale address (the `100.x.y.z` the admin console shows for Home Assistant, a server or a laptop) timed out while the VPN page said connected, unless that device happened to have talked to the WiCAN PRO first; and once the link between the two had been interrupted for about three minutes (Home Assistant restarted, the internet down at either end), the pushes stayed dead until the other device contacted the WiCAN PRO again. Two causes. While no Tailscale session was up, the first packet for such an address never reached the tunnel: the connection attempt left on the WiFi network instead (no data went with it, a connection was never made) and nothing asked for a session. And a session key that had expired with the connection still counted as a live session, so no new one was started. The device now routes the tailnet's own address range into the tunnel at all times and expires that key with its session. Addresses behind a subnet router were not affected (they were fixed in beta.8).
- **WireGuard: a server that stops answering is noticed on a tunnel of any age.** Once a WireGuard tunnel had been up for two minutes, the VPN page kept saying connected for as long as the device ran, even with the server gone: the tunnel was never torn down and set up again, so a server reached by name whose address had changed was not looked up again until a restart. The check behind this only worked on a tunnel younger than two minutes, for the same reason as above: an expired session key still counted as a live session. The tunnel is now reported as down within about three and a half minutes of the server's last answer and reconnects with a growing wait, as it always did on a young tunnel; connections through the tunnel (MQTT, Home Assistant) are opened again once it is back.

## New since beta.7
- **Quick Setup asks to update an OBD chip on firmware V2.3.22.** On that chip firmware the warm start the device sends at every start stops the chip's automatic protocol search until the device is unplugged: apps that use the automatic protocol take minutes to connect or never do, and the vehicle detection cannot find the car (found 2026-11-08 with Car Scanner; V2.3.24 does not have the fault, and 6.0.0-beta.5 had written V2.3.22 onto chips that came with V2.3.24). The vehicle step of Quick Setup now opens with a popup when the chip's firmware is older than the one the device carries: one button, the records written as a bar, Done; a tap beside the popup leaves the same offer as a banner on the step, and the detection waits for the chip. The Status page shows the same banner and popup for a device that was set up before (the popup once per browser session, the banner until the chip is updated). System > Maintenance keeps its Update button. WiCAN stays awake while the chip is written: a running sleep countdown is pushed out a minute at a time (the countdown bar says so, `GET /api/sleep` carries `activity_why`, the `sleep` command prints it) and the sleep entry waits for the whole job, the chip's own restart included.
- **Data destinations: the table reads at a glance.** One status chip per row in a fixed column (Not applied yet, Paused, Sending with the count, Failing with the retry time, Waiting for network, the broker or a network), the row's facts (the networks it is active on, retained, the authentication, the certificate set, the first push) as a line under its topic or URL, Options as a named button that opens a panel with the row's settings and its delivery counters, a restart notice while a saved table is not running yet, and the Links row explains what waits while a link is down.
- **A destination can be active on chosen WiFi networks only, and so can the Home Assistant webhook.** A dropdown with check boxes lists the station networks under Settings > WiFi (All networks is the default; ticking a network clears All, unticking the last one puts All back). A restricted sender waits while WiCAN's WiFi is on another network, or on no WiFi at all (the ESPNetLink or USB Ethernet alone); nothing is counted as a failure meanwhile, and the status says which network it waits for. New `networks` key on both settings (network names separated by commas, empty = all; a name that itself contains a comma cannot be listed), `networks` / `on_network` / `skipped_network` and the station's `ssid` on `GET /api/destinations`, `networks` / `on_network` on `GET /api/webhook`, `ssid` on `GET /api/wifi/status`; the `destinations` and `webhook` commands print them.
- **Store and forward for Home Assistant.** A car away from home keeps recording its vehicle parameters on the SD card, and once it is back on the home network the Home Assistant integration (the `meatpi-multi-device` branch, updated the same day) pulls the recorded hours and imports them into each sensor's long-term statistics at the time they were recorded. One switch on Automate > Home Assistant turns it on (it sets the logger to JSON lines with every AutoPID sample); the card shows what is recorded, the files on the card and when Home Assistant last read the log, and the switch is unavailable without a usable SD card. With a VPN on, the card warns that the integration reaches the device away from home too and pulls the log over mobile data as well. Rows the device wrote before its clock was set (a dead RTC until the first time sync) are placed at their true hours by the integration from the device's clock journal. While Home Assistant reads the log (or the dashboard reads its history) a running sleep countdown is pushed out a minute at a time, ten minutes at most, so the pull finishes before the device sleeps; the countdown bar and the Power Saving page say so. `GET /api/logger` reports the parameter log's format and sink and an `export` block (reads, rows, bytes, the age of the last read and its cursor); `GET /api/logger/export` takes a plain epoch as `since` beside its own cursor; `GET /api/sleep` carries `activity_s`; the `logger` and `sleep` commands print the same. Hourly resolution: the live history detail and the logbook are not replayed. The integration side gains a Catch up history button and two options (the catch-up itself, and whether an hour Home Assistant already has is kept or replaced by the recorded one).
- **The GPS track as a GPX file.** The GPS fix recorded with the vehicle parameters (Store and forward, or the parameter log on) is served back as a GPX 1.1 track by `GET /api/logger/gpx` (a point per fix with elevation, time, speed, course and satellites; a new segment after ten minutes without a fix): the Logger page's GPS track card downloads the last hour, 3, 6 or 24 hours, 7 days or everything on the card, and the Home Assistant integration's `wican.save_gpx` service saves a device's track under `www/wican/` (reachable as `/local/wican/` followed by the file name), since Home Assistant's own history cannot hold past positions; the integration also ships a map card (`custom:wican-gpx-card`) that draws a device's recorded track on Home Assistant's own map.
- **The Bluetooth console log tells a session's story.** A phone app's connection used to leave two lines, `client connected` and `client disconnected`. Each session now logs who connected (the phone's on-air address and type), the connection interval it opened with and the one it settled on, when the device asked it to pair, when the data pipe was subscribed, when the app first wrote, and at the end why the link closed (the phone closed it, the radio link was lost, the device closed it, a stale bond) with the session in numbers: its length, the time to secured, the bytes each way, the last write before the end, drops. One line per milestone, for a support request about an app that "takes four tries" or "connects and shows nothing".
- `GET /api/vpn` reports `derp_region`, the Tailscale relay region the device is reachable at, and the `vpn` console command prints it beside `relay up`.
- The Status page's MQTT row says `Not connected` while MQTT is enabled and the broker cannot be reached, and `Off` only when MQTT is disabled. Before, an unreachable broker read as `Off`, which looks like a disabled setting.

## Bug fixes since beta.7
- **A bonded iPhone or iPad reconnecting over Bluetooth was refused for its first seconds and asked for the PIN again.** iOS encrypts a bonded link within one connection interval, before the Bluetooth stack had told the firmware about the connection, and the firmware then treated the link as plain: the app's first commands were rejected, the device asked the phone to pair once more (the second PIN prompt), and apps such as Car Scanner or RealDash gave up or needed several attempts at every start. The firmware now reads the link's real security state at the connect and never asks an encrypted link to pair. Android phones were not affected (they encrypt later).
- **The OBD chip is told to sleep before its sleep pin is pulled, as firmware 4.x did.** At every sleep entry and at every re-sleep inside a nap the device now sends the chip the ST sleep command (`STSLEEP0`) and pulls the PowerEn pin behind it; v6 pulled the pin alone. The pin works only through the chip vendor's PowerEn function, so a chip whose PowerEn switch was off or inverted never slept under v6: the device reset it six times, rebooted to recover, stayed awake for the whole sleep delay and started over, all night on a low battery. Such a chip sleeps on the command now, and the console logs the chip's acknowledgement at every entry (`chip asleep by command`); a chip that refuses the word (one that is streaming a monitor, for example) gets one warning and the pin as before, and the nap's re-sleep waits for the chip to boot after its reset before sending the word. The command writes nothing to the chip's EEPROM; the wake is unchanged.
- **The device no longer writes voltage thresholds or a UART timeout into the OBD chip.** At start-up the device used to program the chip's own sleep and wake voltages and its UART inactivity timeout from three hidden settings, and to rewrite them whenever the chip's stored values differed, while keeping the chip's own sleep and wake triggers switched off, since the device itself decides when to sleep. A bench proof shows the chip ignores those stored values completely while its triggers are off: it neither sleeps under the stored threshold, nor wakes over it, nor sleeps after the stored UART silence. The start-up now only makes sure the chip's four triggers are off and leaves the stored values alone, so a chip is written less often and the three settings (`wake_voltage_mv`, `sleep_voltage_mv`, `sleep_time_min` under `obd_chip`) are kept but unused.
- **The repair of the chip's PowerEn switch is read back before the reset.** When the device finds the switch set to anything but `10` at start-up it writes `VTPPSW10`; the write is now acknowledged, left 600 ms to settle (as 4.x did) and read back as `10` before the reset pulse that applies it, where before the pulse came the instant the chip said OK. A switch the chip cannot report is left alone, and a refused write gets no reset.
- **The parameter log export could cut a page in the middle of a row and glue the cursor line to it.** `GET /api/logger/export` fills a page up to 64 KB; when that budget ran out exactly at the start of a 1 KB read window (about one page in sixteen) the window went out raw: a row cut in half, the cursor line glued to its end, no line break between. A client stopped paging there, and the Home Assistant integration could import an hour from a part of its rows (seen on the bench). A page now ends at its last complete line and the line that did not fit opens the next page; the integration of the same day treats a page without a cursor line as cut and reads it again a minute later, so it copes with devices still on an earlier build.
- **Motion sensor: `imu.bump` fired about every 600 ms on a parked car, whatever the wake threshold.** Wake on motion fired about 100 times a minute with all three axes flagged, a door slam was indistinguishable, raising the threshold changed nothing, and a logger rule on the event filled the SD card at about 144,000 rows a day. The wake threshold and the motion sensitivity live in a register bank of the sensor that is reachable only while the sensor's internal clock runs, and in the sensor's low-power mode that clock is off: the writes landed at random, an axis whose write was lost kept the sensor's reset threshold of zero and flagged a bump on every sample. The device now keeps the sensor's clock on, reads every threshold back after writing it and leaves wake on motion off if the sensor did not take it. The `imu -x` console command prints the sensor's registers as it holds them.
- **Tailscale: the device opens connections to its peers through the relay now.** An MQTT broker or Home Assistant behind a subnet router at home, with the WiCAN PRO on a phone hotspot, showed the VPN connected and the route listed while MQTT and the webhook timed out. The device never started a WireGuard session for its own traffic (a session only came when the peer contacted the device first, or over a direct path, which two NATs rarely allow), and it sent every relayed packet through its own relay region (Dallas) where a peer homed elsewhere is not. It also replied to connection probes over unrelated paths, which could make the peer mistake a relay address for a working direct endpoint. The device now starts the session itself when it has traffic for a peer, sends through the peer's own relay region (dialled on demand, closed after two minutes idle, up to two beside its home connection), keeps probe replies on their incoming transport and installs a direct address only once a probe has proven it. Relay-only traffic through rekey and recovery to a direct connection pass on the bench. The home region is still Dallas unless the control server assigns another; choosing the nearest by latency is planned.
- **HTTPS servers whose certificate chain ends in a cross-signed root are accepted by the built-in certificate bundle.** Cloudflare, among others, serves its chain under GTS Root R4 as signed by GlobalSign Root CA, a root the bundle no longer carries; the device trusts the chain at the GTS root it does carry now. Before, the Home Assistant webhook behind a Cloudflare tunnel, an HTTPS destination or any other HTTPS client on the device failed against such a server with `No matching trusted root certificate found`, and the workaround was uploading GlobalSign Root CA as a certificate set under Settings > Certificates and selecting it on the card. That workaround keeps working; it is no longer needed.

## New since beta.6
- The Firmware update page loads a release's notes in the browser, straight from the release server, instead of through the device: the device no longer downloads them (they were of no use to it) and the 16 KB limit on a release's notes is gone. A phone without internet sees "could not be loaded" for the notes; the update check and the install are unchanged.
- **Bluetooth behaves as firmware 4.x did again** (Settings > Bluetooth). The device advertises with its fixed hardware address, so presence detection by MAC and apps that remember the address work; the previous betas used a private address that changed at every reconnect and every 15 minutes, which stays available as `address` = private. The device asks the phone to pair the moment it connects (`security_request`, on by default; off for a Windows app with its own pairing dialog), and subscribing to the data pipe needs the paired link, so an OBD app pairs at its first step instead of writing into a link that silently dropped everything. Out of the box the OBD chip is bridged to Bluetooth (the new factory connection `br_ble_obd` on the Connections page; an existing device gets it once at the update unless a connection already uses Bluetooth), so pairing an app is all it takes, as on 4.x; the Bluetooth page warns when Bluetooth is on and no connection carries its data pipe. `GET /api/ble` reports `address`, `address_type` and `security_request`.
- **Tailscale subnet routes.** A network another tailnet node advertises as a subnet router (and the admin console approved) is reachable from the WiCAN PRO now, the way it is from a phone: an MQTT broker or Home Assistant at home behind the home router's Tailscale node can be addressed by its LAN address while the car is away. Up to 8 such networks are taken from the tailnet; an exit node's default route is not. `GET /api/vpn` lists them as `routes`, the `vpn` command prints each with the node it goes through, and a network the device is directly on (at home, on that very LAN) stays local.
- Tailscale reports its relay: `GET /api/vpn` carries `derp` (the relay connection is up), the `vpn` command prints `relay up` or `relay down`, and the `vpn_manager: connected` log line carries the internal RAM left at that moment. Tailscale starts only when the device has 30 KB of internal RAM free at that moment (its own start takes about 20 KB); otherwise it waits, says why on `/api/vpn` (`reason`), in the `vpn` command and on the VPN page, naming what frees memory on that device (Bluetooth off, about 32 KB; the WiFi memory profile set to Lean, about 14 KB; or WireGuard), and starts by itself once memory is free. Its own tasks run on PSRAM stacks now, about 13 KB of internal RAM less while the tunnel is up.
- **Bluetooth and Tailscale can run together.** The Bluetooth controller's code moved from internal RAM to flash, which gives a WiCAN PRO about 19 KB more internal RAM (69 KB free at idle instead of 51 KB on a station-mode device with the dongle, 38 KB with Bluetooth on instead of 14). On the bench, with Bluetooth on the Lean profile, Tailscale starts, reaches its relay and a peer, and the device keeps 18 KB of internal RAM free. Two costs: Bluetooth throughput on the 2M PHY is about a quarter lower (about 370 kbps instead of 480 for notifications beside an active WiFi station; the 1M PHY is unchanged), and while the device erases its internal flash (a settings save, a firmware update) the Bluetooth controller pauses for the erase; a 512 KB file written to the internal flash over a live Bluetooth link still arrived complete.
- Bluetooth has a **memory profile** (Settings > Bluetooth). Lean, the default, runs one phone at a time with the ordinary advertising on the 1M PHY and no scanning, and frees about 3 KB of internal RAM; Full keeps the BLE 5 options (2M and coded PHY, extended advertising) at the full cost; Custom exposes the radio controller's own knobs. A device that had chosen a BLE 5 option keeps it on Full. `GET /api/ble` reports the profile and the controller sizing in use. The Bluetooth host's own task runs on a PSRAM stack as well, about 4.5 KB of internal RAM less while Bluetooth is on (about 30 KB on Full, 27 KB on Lean, measured on the bench unit); pairing, bonding and the bond that survives a reboot are unchanged.
- About 13 KB more internal RAM free at idle (83.8 KB instead of 70.7 KB on the bench unit at the same point after boot): the web server's task and the internal filesystem's writer run on PSRAM stacks now, and the flash work their requests need (a settings save, a file, the crash report, an update upload) runs on a small worker task that exists only while such work is going on. The console's `http` command lists the routes that touched the flash since boot, `system -m` and `GET /api/status` (`health.flash_jobs`) the worker's counters; a device too short of internal RAM to run a flash job latches a `flash_jobs_refused` fault. The script engine's file loads, the firmware updater's bookkeeping and the ESPNetLink pairing store use the same worker, and the vehicle store's writer gave back 6 KB of internal RAM.
- The device checks at every start that the OBD chip cannot wake itself on bus traffic while the device sleeps (the chip's programmable parameter 0F, set once at provisioning since 4.x) and writes it back only when a chip firmware image or a tool had changed it. `GET /api/obd_chip` counts such repairs under `eeprom_guard.pp_restores`; an untouched chip costs one read and no write.
- **The OBD chip's firmware is only ever updated, never rewritten.** The "Rewrite the chip firmware" button is gone from System > Maintenance: the OBD chip card now offers an update only while the chip's firmware is older than the one the device carries (a chip still on V2.3.22 sees "A newer chip firmware is available" with an Update to V2.3.24 button), and no path, not the card, not `POST /api/obd_chip/update`, not `obd_chip update`, writes a chip that already runs that version or a newer one. A chip caught in its download mode by an interrupted write is still recovered by completing the download. The card names the chip's firmware by its version alone and keeps its tools in one order: the state, the update when there is one, the configuration report, the wire tools. The `--force` option and the `force` key are refused with the reason.

## Bug fixes since beta.6
- Fixed Bluetooth apps built for firmware 4.x getting no answer on 6.0: the device no longer asked the phone to pair at the connect, a subscribe to the data pipe went through on an unpaired link, and the app's commands written without response were then dropped without any error (the Discord report of writes refused with insufficient authentication and no notification coming back), and out of the box no connection carried the Bluetooth data pipe at all, so even a paired app got nothing. See the Bluetooth entry under New.
- Fixed a crash when a WireGuard tunnel was torn down while connections still ran through it (two beta.5 crash reports in task `vpn_state`: a double free, an instruction fetch error): the tunnel was taken down from the VPN's own task while the network stack was using the tunnel's connections (an MQTT broker or Home Assistant behind the tunnel). Every teardown is such a moment: the peer silent for 30 s, the WiFi or LTE uplink lost, a Pause VPN network joined. The tunnel's network-stack work now runs on the network stack's own thread, for WireGuard and for Tailscale alike.
- Fixed the OBD chip's start taking a hardware reset and about 4 seconds instead of 1 on roughly every third restart of the device: the chip's answer to the `ATZ` the device sent now and then begins with a few stray bytes, which made the device believe the reset had not happened. The device warm-starts the chip now (`ATWS`, as firmware 4.x did), which answers clean within milliseconds, and reads past stray bytes anyway. The chip is ready about 1.3 seconds sooner at every start.
- Fixed the OBD chip memorising every protocol switch into its EEPROM: the chip's memory function is on after every reset, and under v6 every switch to another protocol that a request then answered on was written to the chip's non-volatile protocol store, even through the RAM-only `ATTP` the device sends. A profile that pins different protocols on its rows (the VW MEB family: Audi Q4 e-tron, Skoda Enyaq and Elroq, the VW ID models, Porsche Taycan) wrote that store on every row change, up to seven times per poll cycle, against a rating of 10,000 writes; firmware 4.x switched the memory function off at every AutoPID start and v6 never did. The device now switches it off at every chip start and again right before every protocol switch it sends itself (AutoPID, the vehicle detection, the UDS tool, J2534, the CAN self-test), so no switch is memorised any more. A connected OBD app's `ATZ` is answered as a warm start (the same banner, about a second sooner, as firmware 4.x did) with the memory function switched off behind it, so the app's own protocol choice no longer writes the store either; the app sees exactly the reply it asked for. `GET /api/obd_chip` counts the words under `eeprom_guard.memory_off` and `app_resets`, and the `obd_chip` console command prints them (#1023).
- Fixed Tailscale on v6 never reaching Tailscale's relay servers (DERP): every relay connection failed its TLS handshake on the relay's extra Ed25519 certificate (`TLS handshake failed: X509 - Requested OID is unknown` in the log), so peers that need a relay never connected while direct paths still did. Fixed every failed relay attempt leaking memory (about 21 KB of PSRAM and a few hundred bytes of internal RAM each, several times a minute): a device with Tailscale on ran out of memory within minutes of a start, name lookups and new connections then failed, and with Bluetooth on the device crashed at its first name lookup, three times in a row, and parked itself (#1024). A relay that cannot be reached is retried with a growing wait (10 s to 5 min) instead of three handshakes every few seconds, the Tailscale client's per-packet log lines moved to debug level, and a device out of internal RAM now fails the name lookup or socket call with an error instead of crashing.
- Fixed a device that could not save any settings page once a connection pointed at a TCP/UDP server or WebSocket path that no longer existed: every Submit answered "Apply failed: br4: unknown endpoint 'udp3333'", the connection could not be removed, and a `boot_errors` fault latched at every start. The device accepts the list, leaves such a connection unbuilt with one warning line and the reason on `/api/bridges`, and the Connections page shows the row with the missing server named: Submit re-creates the server, or the row's trash button removes it. A connection and its new TCP/UDP server or WebSocket path submitted together, the way the Connections page builds them, are accepted now and come up after the restart. The Connections page no longer stages a change just by being opened, a refused save names the settings group it came from, and a connection refused at start because its server is already taken by another connection releases its queues.
